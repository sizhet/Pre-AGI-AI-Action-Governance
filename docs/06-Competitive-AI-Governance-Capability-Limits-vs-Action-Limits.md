# Competitive AI Governance: Capability Limits vs Action Limits

## A Strategic Discussion Note on AI Competition, Regulatory Choice, and Governance Equilibrium

> **AI competition may increase risk. But the same competition may also create pressure to govern dangerous AI action more precisely rather than broadly restricting AI capability development.**

---

## 1. The Strategic Governance Question

AI governance is usually discussed as a safety problem.

It is also a competition problem.

Major AI powers must simultaneously consider:

```text
AI Capability
+
Economic Competitiveness
+
National Security
+
Scientific Leadership
+
Public Safety
+
Systemic Risk
```

This creates a difficult strategic choice.

At a simplified level, governments can emphasize two different forms of AI governance:

```text
Option A
Capability Limits

vs.

Option B
Action Limits
```

The distinction is fundamental.

---

## 2. Capability Limits

Capability-oriented governance attempts to reduce risk by constraining how powerful AI systems may become.

Possible mechanisms include:

```text
Training Restrictions
Compute Thresholds
Model Licensing
Capability Ceilings
Release Restrictions
Frontier-Model Controls
```

The underlying logic is:

```text
Less Powerful AI
        ↓
Less Dangerous Capability
        ↓
Lower Risk
```

This can be reasonable in some exceptional high-risk situations.

But it also creates a strategic cost.

If one country restricts AI capability development while another continues developing advanced AI, the first country may sacrifice:

```text
Scientific Capability
Economic Productivity
Military Capability
Industrial Competitiveness
AI Infrastructure
Technological Leadership
```

Unilateral capability restriction can therefore become expensive.

---

## 3. Action Limits

Action-oriented governance takes a different approach.

Instead of primarily asking:

> How powerful may the AI become?

it asks:

> What may the AI autonomously do?

Relevant controls include:

```text
Agent Identity
Authorization
Permission Boundaries
Target Restrictions
Tool Restrictions
Rate Limits
Search Budgets
Loop Limits
Audit
Verification
Human Escalation
Kill / Revocation
Accountability
```

The underlying logic becomes:

```text
Continue Developing Intelligence
        ↓
Separate Intelligence from Authority
        ↓
Bound Consequential Autonomous Action
        ↓
Reduce Operational Risk
```

This approach attempts to preserve AI capability development while controlling how capability becomes external power.

---

# 4. A Simplified Two-Player Game

Consider two major AI competitors:

```text
Country A
Country B
```

Each faces a simplified strategic choice:

```text
Limit Capability

or

Limit Action
```

The resulting conceptual matrix is:

|                                  | Country B: Capability Limits           | Country B: Action Limits                                         |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Country A: Capability Limits** | Mutual capability restraint            | A may accept a capability disadvantage                           |
| **Country A: Action Limits**     | B may accept a capability disadvantage | Continued capability competition with stronger action governance |

This matrix is deliberately simplified.

Its purpose is not to claim a mathematically proven equilibrium.

It highlights an important strategic asymmetry.

---

# 5. The Unilateral Capability-Restriction Problem

Suppose:

```text
Country A
        ↓
Restricts Advanced AI Development
```

while:

```text
Country B
        ↓
Continues Advanced AI Development
        +
Constrains High-Risk AI Action
```

If Country B can govern consequential action effectively, it may obtain:

```text
Capability Advantage
+
Operational Safety Controls
```

while Country A receives:

```text
Reduced AI Risk
+
Capability Disadvantage
```

This makes unilateral capability restraint strategically difficult to sustain.

The stronger AI becomes as an economic, scientific, and national capability, the larger this opportunity cost may become.

---

# 6. The Competitive Action-Governance Hypothesis

This suggests a research hypothesis:

> **When major competitors place high strategic value on continued AI capability development, unilateral capability restriction becomes increasingly costly. If consequential AI action can instead be credibly bounded, action-based governance may become a more stable competitive arrangement than mutual capability restriction.**

We call this the:

> **Competitive Action-Governance Hypothesis**

The argument is not that action governance is automatically stable.

The argument is that competition may create strong incentives to search for governance mechanisms that preserve capability development.

---

# 7. A Possible Strategic Transition

The resulting dynamic may look like:

```text
AI Competition
        ↓
Capability Becomes Strategically Valuable
        ↓
Unilateral Capability Limits Become Costly
        ↓
Demand for Safety Still Remains
        ↓
Search for Less Capability-Restrictive Governance
        ↓
Authority / Action / Accountability Controls
        ↓
Potential Competitive Governance Convergence
```

This produces a counterintuitive possibility:

> **AI competition may itself create pressure for more precise AI governance.**

Competition can increase risk.

But competition can also make crude capability restriction increasingly unattractive.

---

# 8. From Capability Prisoner's Dilemma to Authority Governance

A common AI-race model resembles a prisoner's dilemma:

```text
Country A fears Country B will continue developing AI.

Country B fears Country A will continue developing AI.

        ↓

Neither wants to slow unilaterally.

        ↓

Capability Race
```

If safety requires both sides to stop advancing AI capability, coordination becomes extremely difficult.

Action governance changes the strategic question.

Instead of requiring:

```text
Both sides stop making AI more capable.
```

coordination can focus on:

```text
Both sides restrict specific dangerous uses
of increasingly capable AI.
```

The coordination object changes from:

```text
Intelligence
```

to:

```text
Authority
+
Action
+
Consequence
```

---

# 9. Why Action Governance May Be Easier to Coordinate

Countries may strongly disagree about:

```text
What is AGI?

How much compute is too much?

How capable should models be allowed to become?

Which benchmark defines dangerous intelligence?
```

But they may find greater common interest around questions such as:

```text
Should autonomous AI conduct unauthorized cyber attacks?

Should AI autonomously control critical infrastructure without accountable authorization?

Should persistent agent swarms operate without attribution?

Should high-impact autonomous actions be auditable?

Should critical AI actions have identifiable responsible principals?
```

These questions concern behavior rather than intelligence level.

That may make them more suitable for international governance.

---

# 10. Action Governance Can Also Be More Observable

Capability restraint can be difficult to verify.

Advanced AI research may involve:

```text
Private Training
Distributed Compute
Algorithmic Improvements
Model Distillation
Fine-Tuning
Open Models
Specialized Models
```

Action governance can sometimes produce more concrete evidence.

Examples include:

```text
Agent Identity
Authorization Records
Permission Profiles
Target Access
Execution Logs
Rate Limits
Incident Reports
Audit Trails
Critical-Action Approvals
```

Not every autonomous action is observable.

But many governance mechanisms can be attached directly to operational infrastructure.

---

# 11. The Third Strategy: Race Without Effective Governance

The two-strategy model is incomplete.

A major competitor may choose:

```text
Develop Fast
+
Deploy Fast
+
Govern Weakly
```

This creates a third strategy:

> **Unbounded Competitive Deployment**

This possibility prevents us from simply declaring action/action governance a Nash equilibrium.

The real strategic space is closer to:

```text
A — Capability Restraint

B — Capability Development
    + Strong Action Governance

C — Capability Development
    + Weak Action Governance
```

Strategy C may produce substantial short-term advantages.

It may therefore remain attractive.

---

# 12. Why the Dangerous Strategy May Persist

Weak governance may appear beneficial when:

```text
Short-Term Advantage
        >
Expected Near-Term Cost of Failure
```

This can occur when:

* accidents are rare,
* damage appears delayed,
* responsibility is unclear,
* attribution is difficult,
* geopolitical competition is intense,
* military applications are exempted,
* enforcement is weak.

Therefore action governance does not automatically solve the AI race problem.

It creates a potentially better strategic option.

---

# 13. The Real Equilibrium Question

The deeper question is therefore:

> **Can action governance become credible enough that major competitors can continue developing advanced AI without requiring broad capability restraint, while still preventing competitive pressure from producing unbounded autonomous deployment?**

That is a more useful governance problem than simply asking whether all competitors will agree to slow AI development.

---

# 14. Credibility Is the Key Variable

For action governance to become strategically stable, it must be credible.

Credibility requires more than policy statements.

It may require:

```text
Clear Prohibited Action Classes
Agent Attribution
Permission Enforcement
Independent Verification
Auditability
Incident Reporting
Liability
Platform Duties
Cross-Border Coordination
Enforcement
```

Without credible implementation:

```text
Action Governance
```

can degrade into:

```text
Action-Governance Theater
```

---

# 15. The Defection Problem

Even if two competitors adopt action governance, one may attempt to defect.

For example:

```text
Publicly:
Strong Action Controls

Privately:
Unrestricted Military Agents
Unbounded Cyber Automation
Hidden Agent Swarms
Weak Internal Controls
```

Therefore international action governance still requires mechanisms for:

```text
Verification
Attribution
Detection
Reciprocity
Incident Investigation
Enforcement
```

The prisoner's dilemma does not disappear.

Its object changes.

---

# 16. Military and Intelligence Exceptions

A particularly difficult case involves national-security systems.

Governments may accept strict civilian AI governance while preserving broad autonomy for:

```text
Military Systems
Cyber Operations
Intelligence Systems
Strategic Infrastructure
```

This could create:

```text
Civilian Action Governance
        +
National-Security Action Race
```

Any serious international framework must eventually confront this asymmetry.

---

# 17. Domestic and International Governance Are Connected

Domestic action governance can strengthen international credibility.

A country that already requires:

```text
Agent Identity
Permission Boundaries
Audit
Incident Reporting
Critical-Action Authorization
```

for high-impact domestic AI systems has infrastructure that can support international commitments.

Therefore:

> **Domestic accountability architecture can become infrastructure for international AI governance.**

---

# 18. Action Governance and Regulatory Competition

Competition does not occur only between countries.

It also occurs between:

```text
Companies
Platforms
Open-Source Ecosystems
Cloud Providers
Research Institutions
```

Capability-heavy regulation can impose large fixed compliance costs.

This may advantage large incumbents.

Action-based governance can potentially reduce this distortion by attaching stronger requirements primarily to consequential deployment.

For example:

```text
Open Research Model
        ↓
Low External Authority
        ↓
Light Governance
```

while:

```text
Persistent Production Agent
        ↓
High External Authority
        ↓
Strong Governance
```

The distinction is economically important.

---

# 19. Regulatory Capture Remains Possible

Action-based governance does not eliminate regulatory capture.

Large companies may still attempt to influence:

```text
Standards
Definitions
Compliance Requirements
Certification
Liability Rules
Enforcement
```

Therefore governance should remain as technology-neutral and actor-neutral as practical.

The principle should be:

> **Comparable autonomous impact should produce comparable governance expectations.**

---

# 20. Model Evaluation Still Matters

Competitive action governance does not imply ignoring model capability.

Model evaluation remains important for:

```text
Risk Forecasting
Security Preparation
Emerging Capability Detection
Deployment Planning
AIT Reassessment
```

The relationship remains:

> **Model evaluation informs governance.**

But:

> **Action capability triggers governance.**

And:

> **Consequence determines governance intensity.**

---

# 21. The Autonomous Impact Threshold as a Neutral Trigger

The **Autonomous Impact Threshold (AIT)** provides a useful bridge between competition and governance.

Instead of asking whether a system is:

```text
American
Chinese
Open
Closed
Frontier
Non-Frontier
AGI
Non-AGI
```

ask:

```text
How autonomous is it?

What authority does it have?

What can it access?

At what scale can it act?

How persistent is it?

What consequences can it produce?

What verification exists?
```

This creates a more neutral governance trigger.

---

# 22. AIT Can Reduce Strategic Asymmetry

Suppose both competitors agree that:

```text
AIT-0 / AIT-1
        ↓
Light Governance

AIT-2
        ↓
Strong Operational Controls

AIT-3
        ↓
Strict Accountability
+
Independent Verification
+
Critical-Action Governance
```

Neither country must agree to stop developing more capable AI.

Instead they agree that increasingly consequential autonomous deployment requires increasingly strong controls.

This may be easier to sustain.

---

# 23. Competition Can Produce Better Governance Technology

There is another possibility.

If countries refuse to surrender AI capability advantage but still fear AI accidents and misuse, they gain incentives to develop better:

```text
Verification
Agent Identity
Permission Systems
Runtime Monitoring
Counter-Evidence
Audit
Kill Mechanisms
Structural Localization
Accountability Infrastructure
```

Competition may therefore occur not only in:

```text
Who has the strongest AI?
```

but eventually in:

```text
Who can safely deploy the strongest AI?
```

This would be a healthier competitive dimension.

---

# 24. From AI Capability Race to AI Control Race

A desirable transformation would be:

```text
AI Capability Race
        ↓
AI Capability + Control Race
```

Countries and companies would compete on:

```text
Capability
+
Reliability
+
Verification
+
Safety
+
Controllability
+
Accountability
```

The winner would not simply possess the strongest intelligence.

The winner would possess the strongest **deployable and governable intelligence**.

---

# 25. Strategic Value of Structural Control

This changes the role of AI governance.

Governance stops being merely:

```text
Cost imposed on AI development
```

and can become:

```text
Infrastructure enabling
high-confidence AI deployment
```

A country with better AI Action Control infrastructure may safely authorize more useful AI activity than a country relying on crude capability restrictions.

Therefore:

> **Good governance can become a competitive advantage rather than merely a competitive burden.**

---

# 26. The Strategic Stability Hypothesis

This suggests a broader possibility:

> **Action-based governance may improve strategic stability because it allows continued capability development while creating shared constraints around high-impact autonomous behavior.**

This remains a hypothesis.

Its validity depends on:

* enforceability,
* observability,
* reciprocity,
* attribution,
* trust,
* institutional quality,
* technological control mechanisms.

But it deserves serious consideration.

---

# 27. What Could Break the Hypothesis?

Several conditions could cause competitive action governance to fail.

### Failure Mode 1 — Action Controls Are Technically Weak

Agents bypass controls.

### Failure Mode 2 — Verification Is Impossible

Competitors cannot determine whether commitments are being followed.

### Failure Mode 3 — Short-Term Advantage Dominates

Unsafe deployment produces immediate strategic benefits.

### Failure Mode 4 — Attribution Fails

Nobody can reliably identify who caused harmful autonomous action.

### Failure Mode 5 — National-Security Exceptions Dominate

Most consequential systems are exempted.

### Failure Mode 6 — Catastrophic Model Capability Emerges

A future capability may become so dangerous that upstream capability controls are justified regardless of deployment architecture.

Therefore this note does not argue that action governance will always replace capability governance.

---

# 28. Capability Governance May Still Be Necessary

Certain future technologies may justify upstream controls if:

```text
Capability Itself
        ↓
Creates Extremely High
and Difficult-to-Contain Risk
```

The framework therefore does not assert:

> Never regulate capability.

It asserts:

> **Do not regulate capability merely because capability is easier to measure than consequential action.**

The governance target should follow the actual risk pathway.

---

# 29. A More Precise International Strategy

A layered international approach could therefore be:

```text
Layer 1
Model Evaluation
        ↓
Understand Emerging Capability

Layer 2
Agent Governance
        ↓
Control Runtime Autonomy

Layer 3
Authority Governance
        ↓
Control Permissions and Scale

Layer 4
Action Governance
        ↓

        ↓

```

This allows continued AI development while strengthening control as intelligence approaches real-world consequence.

---

# 30. The Competitive Governance Principle

The central strategic principle of this note is:

> **When capability restraint creates large unilateral competitive costs, governance should seek the closest effective control point to consequential action.**

This does not guarantee cooperation.

But it changes the strategic structure of cooperation.

---

# 31. A Possible Competitive Governance Equilibrium

The desired outcome is not:

```text
Country A stops AI development.
Country B stops AI development.
```

Nor is it:

```text
Country A races without controls.
Country B races without controls.
```

A potentially more stable outcome is:

```text
Country A
Advanced AI Development
+
Strong Action Governance

Country B
Advanced AI Development
+
Strong Action Governance
```

This can be described as a:

> **Competitive Governance Equilibrium**

It should currently be treated as a research hypothesis rather than a proven Nash equilibrium.

---

# 32. From Mutual Restraint to Mutual Boundaries

Traditional arms-control thinking often emphasizes:

```text
Mutual Restraint
```

AI may sometimes require a different model:

```text
Mutual Development
+
Mutual Action Boundaries
```

The distinction reflects an unusual property of AI:

> Intelligence can generate enormous peaceful economic and scientific value while also enabling dangerous autonomous action.

A governance system should preserve the former while constraining the latter.

---

# 33. The Deeper Strategic Insight

The usual conclusion is:

> Competition makes AI safety harder.

That is true.

But it may be incomplete.

A second-order effect is possible:

> **Competition can make broad capability restriction strategically unstable, thereby increasing pressure to develop more precise action-based safety mechanisms.**

Thus:

```text
Competition
        ↓
Capability Restraint Becomes Expensive
        ↓
Safety Problem Remains
        ↓
Better Control Mechanisms Become Valuable
        ↓
Action Governance Becomes Strategically Attractive
```

This is the central insight of this discussion note.

# 34. Relationship to Pre-AGI AI Action Governance

This note extends the main framework of the repository.

The core repository asks:

> **What should be governed?**

This note asks:

> **Why might competing states have incentives to choose that governance target?**

The answer may be:

> Because governing autonomous authority can preserve the strategic benefits of AI capability development while reducing the risks created when intelligence becomes consequential action.

# 35. Five Strategic Principles

The argument can be summarized in five principles.

### Principle 1

> **Unilateral capability restraint can create significant strategic cost.**

### Principle 2

> **Action governance can preserve capability development while constraining consequential autonomous behavior.**

### Principle 3

> **Action/action governance may become a competitive governance equilibrium, but it is not automatically a Nash equilibrium.**

### Principle 4

> **The major competing strategy is rapid capability development combined with weak action governance.**

### Principle 5

> **The long-term challenge is to make strong action governance more strategically attractive than unbounded autonomous deployment.**

# 37. Conclusion

AI competition creates a genuine governance dilemma.

If major competitors believe advanced AI is economically, scientifically, and strategically important, unilateral capability restriction may become increasingly difficult to sustain.

But abandoning governance is not a satisfactory alternative.

This creates space for another strategy:

> **Continue developing intelligence while strictly governing consequential autonomous authority and action.**

The resulting strategic structure may be:

```text
AI Competition
        ↓
Capability Development Continues
        ↓
Safety Pressure Continues
        ↓
Action Governance Becomes Valuable
        ↓
Authority Is Bounded
        ↓
Consequential Action Becomes Accountable
```

Whether this becomes a stable international equilibrium remains uncertain.

The main competing outcome is equally clear:

```text
Develop Fast
+
Deploy Fast
+
Govern Weakly
```

The central international governance problem is therefore not merely how to convince competitors to slow AI development.

It is:

> **How can consequential AI action be made sufficiently bounded, attributable, verifiable, and accountable that continued capability competition does not require uncontrolled autonomous deployment?**

This leads to a broader possibility:

> **The future AI competition may not only be a race to build the strongest intelligence. It may also become a race to build the strongest intelligence that can be safely controlled and responsibly deployed.**

If that transition occurs, AI governance will no longer be merely a constraint on technological competition.

It will become part of technological competitiveness itself.

---

## Strategic Summary

> **Capability competition makes unilateral "limits on intelligence" costly.**

> **Action governance offers a possible alternative: preserve intelligence development while bounding autonomous authority.**

> **The resulting action/action outcome is a plausible competitive governance equilibrium, not yet a proven Nash equilibrium.**

> **The real challenge is preventing defection into rapid development plus weak action control.**

> **Good AI governance may ultimately become a competitive advantage.**
